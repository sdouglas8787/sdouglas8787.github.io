The unglamorous machinery

Why pricing programmes fail without governance, and what the machinery actually is.

Stuart Douglas · November 2026

Most pricing problems are not technology problems. They are governance, discipline and adoption problems. The technology just makes the fix scalable.

I learned this running a programme in a large industrial business where most orders were priced by hand. Manual pricing is where discount discipline goes to die: there is no reference to deviate from, so there is nothing to audit, and no way to see leakage. Over the programme, manual pricing fell from over 80% of orders to under a quarter, and the share of products with no list price at all fell by more than half. The margin also improved over the same period. Several other things moved at once, so I would not claim the margin as a pricing result, and I will come back to why that sentence belongs in an essay about governance.

The transferable part was never the numbers. It was the machinery.

What the machinery is

Five parts, none of them glamorous.

A reference price. You cannot govern deviation from a price that does not exist. Before corridors, before dashboards, before any of it: every product that is sold needs a price it is notionally sold at. In businesses with long tails and configured products this is the hardest and least rewarding step, which is why it is usually skipped.

A corridor. Discretion is not the enemy; unbounded discretion is. A corridor says how far a price can move from the reference and on whose say-so. The width is a commercial judgement and should be argued about openly. What matters is that stepping outside it is a decision someone takes, not a thing that happens.

An audit with teeth. Delegation of authority only works when somebody checks, on a cadence, by name. A quarterly review that reads actual discounts against the authority that approved them turns the corridor from a slide into a control. The first few audits are uncomfortable. That is how you know they are working.

A measure of landing. Most pricing functions can tell you the price they agreed. Far fewer can tell you the price they achieved, because the two live in different systems and the gap belongs to nobody. A price increase that was agreed but never implemented is not a price increase, and until you measure that, nobody has to admit it. Realisation turns an opinion into a number people can argue about, which is the entire point of a KPI.

A residual that gets reported. Every gross-to-net bridge has a share it cannot explain. The common practice is to fold it into whichever bar is nearest, usually the one labelled other. The governed practice is to derive it and print it, every time, as the bridge's own error rate. At 0.2% of gross it is a rounding story. At 3% it means the deduction taxonomy is incomplete and every conversation about trade investment is running on unchecked numbers. The residual is the most honest number in pricing, which is presumably why it is so rarely published.

Governing your own claims

There is a sixth part, and it is the one programmes most often fail: governance applies to the attribution, not just the prices. When a margin moves during a pricing programme, the programme will be given credit, because the number and the confounds live in different places and only the number makes it into the deck. Mix moves. Low-margin volume falls out. Lines get rationalised. If the programme cannot show a like-for-like comparison, the honest statement is that the controlled metrics moved and the margin moved alongside them. Saying so costs a slide's worth of applause and buys something better: nobody can take the claim apart later, because it was never inflated.

Why programmes fail anyway

Two reasons, in my experience, and neither is the software.

The first is that governance arrives as refusal. You get resistance when pricing is just the function that says no. A corridor done well is a speed-up, not a brake: inside it, nobody needs permission, and the sales team gets a faster yes than they ever got from case-by-case escalation. If the governed path is slower than the ungoverned one was, the machinery will be routed around, and it will deserve it.

The second is definitions. Sales, finance and pricing arrive with three different numbers for the same measure, everyone concludes the data is bad, and the programme stalls in reconciliation. It usually is not the data. Nobody agreed what a customer is, what a product is, or which date counts. No governance survives undefined terms, because every audit finding dissolves into an argument about the basis.

The evidence this is not just taste

The research points the same way. Liozu and Hinterhuber surveyed 1,812 pricing professionals and found that pricing capabilities, the organisational machinery rather than the price points, are positively related to firm performance, and that how a firm prices matters as much as what it charges. Hinterhuber's earlier work documents the default this machinery exists to resist: left ungoverned, industrial firms drift to cost-plus and systematically underprice what differentiates them. Governance is not administration. It is how a pricing strategy survives contact with a quarter-end.

None of this is exciting to build. A reference price nobody thanks you for, a corridor people initially resent, an audit that makes enemies before it makes habits, a KPI that announces your own shortfalls, and a residual that advertises what you cannot explain. The machinery is boring on purpose. What it buys is the right to be believed, and in pricing that is the scarcest asset there is.
