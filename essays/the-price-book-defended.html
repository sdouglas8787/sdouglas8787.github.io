The price book, defended

A strategic direction for list pricing: what evidence a price must survive, and where pricing functions go after the blanket increase.

Stuart Douglas · November 2026

Ask who decided today's list price for any given product and the honest answer, in most businesses, is nobody. Somebody decided a price years ago. Every price since has been that decision multiplied by a series of blanket increases, each reasonable at the time, none of them a fresh look. The price book is not a set of decisions. It is a set of histories, compounding.

This is the default, not a scandal. A book of thousands of lines cannot be hand-reviewed annually, and the blanket increase exists because it scales. The strategic question is not how to abolish it but what replaces habit as the basis when a price does get looked at. My answer: a list price is defensible when it can survive four questions.

Four questions a list price must survive

  1. What do buyers actually pay now? Not what the book says: what recent transactions landed at, net. A list price far above every achieved price is not ambition, it is fiction, and the sales force prices off the fiction's reputation rather than its number.
  2. What do the best-positioned buyers pay? Different segments pay differently for the same product, and the dispersion is the most informative thing in the transaction data. The upper end of what comparable buyers genuinely pay is a better anchor for a list price than any cost build-up, because somebody real is paying it.
  3. What floor does the economics set? Cost plus the minimum margin the product line must earn. Below that, a list price is a standing invitation to sell at a loss once normal discounting does its work. The floor is not a price recommendation. It is the line under the argument.
  4. When was this price last deliberately decided? A price that has only ever been escalated carries its original error, compounded. A price that fell, and that nobody remembers cutting, is a finding on its own. Decision dates belong in the book next to the prices.

None of this is exotic. What makes it strategic is doing it systematically, on a cadence, with named ownership, instead of heroically, once, in a spreadsheet that retires with its author.

Dispersion is information

A product sold in many markets will carry different prices in each, and the instinct is to read the spread as error to be harmonised away. Mostly it is not. Markets differ in willingness to pay, logistics, tax and competitive position, and the spread encodes all of it. The governed question is not why are these different but can each difference say why it exists. A price that cannot explain its distance from its siblings is the one that needs the review. Harmonisation that flattens explained differences destroys information and margin together.

Announced is not achieved

The strategic failure of the blanket increase is not that it is blanket. It is that the announced number gets budgeted as if it were the achieved number. Increases erode on their way to the invoice: contracts reprice later, discounts absorb, mix shifts. A pricing function that budgets realised growth, using its own measured history of how much of an announced increase typically lands, will stop surprising its finance team twice a year. One number, honestly discounted by its own track record, is worth more than any amount of announcement.

Where this goes next

The direction of travel for pricing functions is a maturity sequence, and most of the industry is still on the first step.

Habit: cost-plus where there is a cost, history-plus where there is not, blanket increases throughout. Mochtar and Arditi's survey of large contractors found markup set mostly by intuition after a subjective read of the competition, and that description travels well beyond construction.

Evidence: the four questions, asked on a cadence, with the transaction data doing the answering. For engineered and configured products, where no two orders are identical, Kingsman and de Souza made the essential reframe decades ago: quotation pricing is a repeated statistical decision, not a one-off calculation, and it improves when win-rate history feeds back into the markup. The price book still matters there, as the anchor every quote deviates from.

Experiment: the step almost nobody has taken. Evidence from history can only show what happened under the prices you had. Dubé and Misra's pricing experiment at scale showed the gap between the two: the firm's existing price left roughly half the available profit unclaimed, and the result was only knowable because prices were varied deliberately and validated before rollout. Models nominate; experiments decide. For most industrial pricing functions this is a five-year ambition, and that is precisely why it belongs in the strategy now.

A price book defended this way changes the texture of pricing conversations. Increases stop being announcements and become cases. Cuts stop being invisible and become decisions. And when a customer, an auditor or a new commercial director asks why a price is what it is, there is an answer that does not begin with history.
